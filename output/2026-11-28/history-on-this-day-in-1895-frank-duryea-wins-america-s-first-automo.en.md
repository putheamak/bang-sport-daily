# On This Day in 1895: Frank Duryea Wins America's First Automobile Race in Chicago Snow

*Battling blizzards, breakdowns and horse tram collisions, Duryea averaged 5 mph across 54 miles to claim the Times-Herald purse.*

**On this day** · 2026-11-28

On November 28, 1895, American competitive motorsport began in the snow and mud of Chicago when six horseless vehicles started the Chicago Times-Herald race. The 54-mile (87 km) contest ran from Jackson Park to Evanston and back, testing early road machines through slush and snow drifts at 38 °F (4 °C). Frank Duryea drove his petrol-powered Duryea Motor Wagon across the finish line first near the Palace of Fine Arts after 10 hours and 23 minutes, claiming the $2,000 first prize with an average overall speed of 5.0 mph.

## Snow drifts and six starters in Jackson Park

Hermann H. Kohlsaat, proprietor of the Chicago Times-Herald, announced the event on July 10, 1895, offering a total purse of $5,000 to stimulate domestic automobile engineering and increase newspaper circulation. Seeking an alternative to the cumbersome phrase "horseless carriage," the newspaper sponsored a $500 naming contest that selected the label "motocycle". Organizers initially mapped a 100-mile course to Milwaukee, but deep mud and unpaved roads forced a revision to Waukegan and a target date of November 2.

When only two cars were ready on November 2, the paper converted the run into a 100-mile exhibition for a $500 prize. Oscar B. Mueller won that purse in a German Benz Vis-à-vis after Duryea swerved into a ditch at Prairie View to avoid a farmer's wagon, wrecking a wheel. Municipal authorities also halted cars entering Chicago, declaring that motor wagons possessed no legal right to use city streets without horses. The race was postponed to Thanksgiving Day while the newspaper successfully lobbied the Chicago city council to pass an ordinance legalizing self-propelled road vehicles.

## Forges, frostbite and horse sleighs on the road to Evanston

Eighty-three entries had registered, but incomplete builds, transit delays, and mechanical failures reduced the Thanksgiving field to six. Early favorite Elwood Haynes suffered vehicle damage while driving to Chicago and withdrew. The final starting grid featured four gasoline vehicles—Duryea's wagon and three Benz carriages imported through French agent Emile Roger—alongside two battery-powered entries: a Sturges Electro Motocycle and a Morris & Salom Electrobat.

The electric cars, lacking arranged roadside charging stations, stalled on the course's grades and succumbed to freezing temperatures. Shortly after departing Jackson Park, one Benz slipped on wet tram tracks and collided with a horse-drawn streetcar, forcing its retirement. Duryea started at 8:55 a.m. but suffered two breakdowns that required stops at local blacksmith shops for forge repairs. Having designed and constructed the vehicle himself, Duryea completed the repairs and resumed driving. Over the final 12 miles from Douglas Park, he followed a horse-drawn sleigh that broke a path through deep drifts.

Mueller's Benz started at 10:06 a.m. with twine wrapped around its solid-rubber tires and sanded drive belts to reduce slippage. As night fell, Mueller collapsed from cold exposure. Race umpire Charles Brady King took the steering lever at course point 31 and guided the Benz across the finish line at 8:53 p.m. Mueller was awarded second place with an elapsed time of 10 hours 47 minutes (running time 9 hours 32 minutes) at an average speed of 4.9 mph.

## From a 5 mph crawl to an American industry

Duryea crossed the line at 7:18 p.m., recording a running time of 7 hours and 53 minutes, or 6.7 mph (11 km/h) while in motion. Only the Duryea and Mueller's Benz finished the course. King later wrote that the contest proved the operational superiority of pneumatic tires over solid rubber on winter roads.

The Thanksgiving race demonstrated that internal combustion engines could function in harsh winter climates where electric batteries failed. The publicity and the $2,000 winner's check gave Frank and his brother Charles Duryea the capital needed to commercialize the Duryea Motor Wagon Company. Their victory established the viability of American automobile manufacturing and marked the starting point of organized motor racing in the United States.
