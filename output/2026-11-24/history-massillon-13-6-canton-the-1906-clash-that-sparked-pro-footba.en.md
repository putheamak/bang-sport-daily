# Massillon 13-6 Canton: The 1906 Clash That Sparked Pro Football's First Scandal

*On November 24, 1906, the Massillon Tigers secured the Ohio title before bribery charges brought both powerhouse franchises to the brink of ruin.*

**On this day** · 2026-11-24

On November 24, 1906, the Massillon Tigers defeated the Canton Bulldogs 13–6 in Massillon, Ohio, claiming the Ohio League championship in the second contest of a two-game post-season series. The final whistle concluded an intense duel between two clubs based just 15 miles apart in Stark County, but it immediately triggered the first major match-fixing and betting controversy in American professional football history.

Eight days earlier, on November 16, Canton had captured the opening encounter 10–5 at Mahaffey Park in Canton, inflicting Massillon's first defeat of the season. Both clubs had spent heavily to sign prominent players from across the country, with Canton hiring Massillon's entire 1905 backfield during the off-season. To ensure neither side defaulted, each organization posted a $3,000 bond, while gate receipts were locked in bank escrow accounts in Canton and Massillon. Interest was so high that Bell Telephone linemen monitored the sidelines to telegraph play-by-play updates across the country. Massillon's 13–6 victory in the second game leveled the series and retained the state title.

## The Bribery Allegations and Hotel Brawl

The athletic achievement was overshadowed before the weekend ended. On the night of November 24, 1906, a brawl erupted inside the bar of Canton's Courtland Hotel and spilled into the streets, involving Canton players and supporters who alleged that members of the Bulldogs had deliberately lost the game.

Public accusations followed quickly in print. Massillon manager Ed Stewart, a journalist with newspaper ties, published charges in the *Massillon Independent* stating that end Walter East and Canton player-coach Charles Edgar "Blondy" Wallace had conspired to rig the series. According to Stewart's account, East claimed to possess $50,000 in gambler backing and had attempted to bribe Massillon teammates Robert "Tiny" Maxwell and Bob Shiring to throw the opening leg.

Maxwell and Shiring reported the illicit proposal to Massillon head coach Sherburn Wightman, prompting the club to release East before the second game took place. Rumors and competing accounts spread rapidly. One theory maintained that Wallace and East had orchestrated a split across the two games to force a high-drawing third rubber match in Cleveland, where gate receipts would reach record heights. Other accounts alleged that Canton players had wagered heavily on themselves before Wallace altered the plan.

## Lawsuits, Insolvency, and the Path to the NFL

Wallace denied any wrongdoing, insisting that Massillon fabricated the story to damage Canton's reputation and ruin gate receipts for an upcoming Thanksgiving exhibition game against Pennsylvania powerhouse Latrobe. In response, Wallace filed a $25,000 libel lawsuit against Stewart and the newspaper. The defamation suit never proceeded to a courtroom verdict and was settled out of court, leaving the exact facts of the purported agreement unresolved.

The reputational damage and financial strain proved catastrophic for professional gridiron in Ohio. Canton finished the 1906 season roughly $6,000 short of meeting its player payroll obligations. The Canton Athletic Club collapsed financially and failed to field a team in 1907. Massillon operated an amateur squad under the name "All-Massillons" in 1907 before also folding.

The Stark County rivals did not play each other again until 1911. The regional football scene eventually rebounded from the collapse, serving as the foundation for modern American football. On September 17, 1920, representatives from Canton, Massillon, Akron, and several other regional clubs gathered in an automobile showroom in Canton to form the American Professional Football Association, the league that was subsequently renamed the National Football League.
