# On This Day in 1975: UN General Assembly Passes Resolution 3379

*A look back at the historic November 10 vote that determined Zionism to be a form of racism, its deep roots in the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, and its eventual repeal sixteen years later.*

**On this day** · 2026-11-10

On November 10, 1975, the United Nations General Assembly gathered in New York to vote on a measure that would become a historic flashpoint in international diplomacy. By a vote of 72 to 35, with 32 abstentions, the General Assembly passed Resolution 3379, a text that determined Zionism to be a form of racism and racial discrimination.

The decision marked a profound moment of contention within the international body, reflecting deep geopolitical realignments of the era. The United Nations General Assembly, serving as the main deliberative and representative organ of the UN, had increasingly become a platform where developing nations and shifting voting blocs could shape global debates. Resolution 3379 emerged directly out of the broader, decades-long Israeli-Palestinian conflict over land, self-determination, and refugees that traced its roots back to late 19th-century political Zionism and the 1917 Balfour Declaration issued by Britain.

## The Diplomatic Divide and Geopolitical Realities

The passage of Resolution 3379 intensified polarization within international institutions. Critics of the resolution argued that it equated national liberation movements and self-determination with bigotry, while supporters maintained that the measure addressed systemic discrimination and the displacement of the Palestinian population, particularly following the 1948 Palestine war and the 1967 Six-Day War.

During the decades preceding and following the vote, the makeup of the United Nations had undergone remarkable changes. In 1945, the United Nations had 51 founding members, but by the later decades of the 20th century, membership nearly quadrupled to 193 nations, of which more than two-thirds were developing countries. Because of their numbers, developing countries and coordinating groups were frequently able to determine the agenda of the Assembly, the character of its debates, and the nature of its decisions. For many developing countries, the UN served as the primary source of diplomatic influence and the principal outlet for foreign relations initiatives.

While General Assembly resolutions are generally non-binding statements symbolizing the sense of the international community—unlike the enforcement powers held by the UN Security Council—the symbolic weight of Resolution 3379 resonated globally for more than a decade. It became a focal point of intense diplomatic friction, dividing Western nations from the coalition of Arab and developing states that dominated much of the assembly's agenda during the North-South dialogues of the 1980s.

## Legacy and Eventual Repeal

The controversy surrounding Resolution 3379 persisted until the early 1990s, coinciding with shifting diplomatic dynamics in the Middle East, including the Madrid Peace Conference. In December 1991, the United Nations General Assembly passed Resolution 46/86, which revoked the determination made sixteen years earlier. The repeal passed with 111 votes in favor, 25 against, and 13 abstentions, effectively nullifying the 1975 text.

Decades later, the legacy of Resolution 3379 remains a notable case study in the intersection of international law, politics, and diplomacy at the United Nations. It illustrates how the world body's central deliberative chamber has served not only as a forum for consensus-building, but also as a theater for the deepest political struggles of the twentieth century, echoing the ongoing complexities of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict that continue to shape global affairs.
